# Candidates — One Line, One Laugh: Roast Your Fridge

Judge brief (from RULES.txt), criteria in weight order:

1. Roasts a fridge or a phone in a single line.
2. Lands a real laugh on a specific, recognisable detail of the appliance rather than a generic insult.
3. Turns on a twist, a surprise or a double meaning that pays off in the last few words.
4. Reads cleanly aloud as one line, with no setup that needs a second sentence.
5. Feels fresh rather than a joke everyone who owns a fridge has already worn out.

Every match below was decided by the first criterion where the two lines differ (a later criterion only decides when it differs by much more), exactly as the published brief instructs the house judge.

## Round 1 (8 matches)

- **M1: C13 beats C2.** C2's payoff ("that's what my ice tastes like") is a whole clause, and the twist is mild. C13's double-meaning payoff ("still asks if I'm a robot") is sharper on criterion 3.
- **M2: C12 beats C10.** C10 dies on criterion 5: the autocorrect-"ducking" gag is the most worn-out phone joke there is. C12's "become a better person" twist is fresh.
- **M3: C4 beats C7.** C7's payoff ("zero plans... describes my whole life") is generic self-deprecation, weak on criterion 2. C4's jar-versus-relationships is a specific, recognisable detail.
- **M4: C1 beats C15.** C15's "embarrassed to be seen with me" is a generic insult, weak on criterion 2. C1's sentient spinach is a specific detail with a surprise payoff.
- **M5: C14 beats C6.** C6's "mystery meat" is a familiar freezer trope, weak on criterion 5. C14's "only honest performance review" twist is fresher and sharper.
- **M6: C3 beats C11.** C11's payoff ("the document I actually need") is mild on criteria 2–3. C3's beep-as-bill-payer personification lands harder.
- **M7: C5 beats C16.** C16's snooze joke is generic behaviour, weak on criterion 2. C5's red filter light versus a check-engine light is a specific, recognisable detail.
- **M8: C9 beats C8.** C8 is 127 characters and its payoff ("never once made me a sandwich") is a clause, not a snap. C9's "most dramatic exit since my last relationship" pays off in the last words, winning on criteria 3 and 4.

## Quarter-finals

- **QF1: C13 beats C12 (margin 2).** Both strong. Criterion 2: C13's three-part biometric list versus the CAPTCHA is more appliance-specific than C12's storage of self-improvement apps. Criterion 3: C13's twist is a genuine double meaning — the device that knows you are human asks if you are a robot — while C12's "never opened" is ironic but predictable once "become a better person" lands.
- **QF2: C4 beats C1 (margin 1).** Both surprise payoffs. Criterion 5 decides: rotting spinach in the crisper is a more familiar trope than a jar outlasting three relationships, so C4's framing is fresher.
- **QF3: C14 beats C3 (margin 2).** Criterion 3: C14's "only honest performance review" is a sharper, more surprising reframe than C3's "paying the electric bill" personification, which the reader sees coming halfway through the line.
- **QF4: C9 beats C5 (margin 1).** Criteria 2–3: C9's 20%-battery dramatic exit is a punchier, more surprising payoff; C5's check-engine analogy is clever but the laugh is milder.

## Semi-finals

- **SF1: C13 beats C4 (margin 2).** Criterion 3: C13's double meaning inverts an expectation nobody had questioned — the all-knowing phone doubting you are human. C4's payoff leans on the familiar "my love life is a mess" frame, which costs it against a genuinely surprising twist.
- **SF2: C14 beats C9 (margin 1).** Criterion 3: C14's "only honest performance review" reframes the whole line; C9's "dramatic exit since my last relationship" is a good joke, but the comparison is a slightly generic one.

## Final

- **C13 beats C14 (margin 2).** Criterion 1: tie, both roast a phone in one line. Criterion 2: C13's detail (face, fingerprint, location versus the CAPTCHA) is more appliance-specific than C14's screen-time report. Criterion 3: both twist in the last words, but C13's is a true double meaning while C14's is an ironic reframe. Criterion 4: C13 reads cleaner aloud — 83 characters, a list rhythm, a snap ending. C14 is the worthy runner-up.

## Champion

> My phone knows my face, my fingerprint, my location, and still asks if I'm a robot.

83 characters. Tournament record: 4–0 (M1 vs C2, QF1 vs C12, SF1 vs C4, Final vs C14).

## Why the champion wins — against the brief, in order

1. **Roasts a phone in a single line.** Unmistakably a phone: face unlock, fingerprint, location tracking, CAPTCHA. It could not be about anything else.
2. **Specific, recognisable detail.** Not "my phone is slow" or "my phone is old" — the exact trio of biometric/location knowledge every smartphone owner carries, set against the "I'm not a robot" checkbox every reader has clicked a hundred times.
3. **Twist in the last few words.** "Still asks if I'm a robot" is a double meaning: the literal CAPTCHA question, and the absurd verdict on a person the phone already knows inside out. The all-knowing device is the one sceptic in the room.
4. **Reads cleanly aloud.** One sentence, a three-item list building to a snap payoff, no setup needing a second sentence.
5. **Fresh.** The CAPTCHA-versus-biometrics inversion is not a joke anyone has worn out — the familiar pieces (face unlock, CAPTCHA) are combined in a way nobody had put together.
